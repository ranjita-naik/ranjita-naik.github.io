Show me first

4 August 2026

You ask for something ordinary. Go through the renewals list and email everyone whose contract ends this quarter.

It doesn’t do it.

What comes back is a plan.

  1. Identify customers whose contracts end this quarter.
  2. Draft one renewal email per customer.
  3. Review the emails.
  4. Send the emails.
  5. Report back what went out.

Then it stops, and waits for you.

You asked it to do the work. It answered with a proposal instead.

The first time this happens, it feels like hesitation. Or like being asked for permission you didn’t know you were withholding.

Where the plan came from

Those five steps didn’t appear out of nowhere.

Before writing the plan, the agent spent a few moments understanding the job. It looked through the renewals list, checked the contract dates, and built a picture of what needed to happen. From that, it wrote the five-step plan you just saw.

Nothing has been changed yet. No emails have been drafted or sent. The only thing it has produced is the plan. When you approve it, the work begins.

Treat the plan like a draft

Those five steps weren’t copied from a manual. They came from the agent’s understanding of the job.

That means they’re informed. It also means they can be wrong.

The first instinct is usually to skim the plan, decide it looks sensible, and press Yes. Treating it that way makes the plan feel like a verdict.

It isn’t. It’s the first draft of how the work will be done.

Say what’s wrong in ordinary words — these customers have already renewed, check the invoices before sending anything, hold the emails until Monday — and the plan changes before any work begins. In Claude Code you can even open the plan directly in a text editor and edit it before anything runs.

That settles what kind of thing a plan is. It opens in a text editor. Whatever else it might be, it’s a document.

Your edits The work that followed

The plan is a draft, not a decision.

From plan to work

The plan isn’t the work. It is the last chance to change the work before it begins.

Once you are happy with it, ask the agent to carry it out. It will follow the version you approved — whether that is its original proposal or the version you revised.

You do not need to copy the plan somewhere else or turn it into another prompt. In Claude Code, approval starts the work. In an ordinary chat, ask the agent to carry out the agreed plan.

What it costs you

Planning is not free. You read something, you decide something, and none of the work has happened yet. On a small job, that is the entire cost with none of the benefit.

Planning earns its keep when you are unsure of the approach, when the work touches several files, or when you do not yet understand the thing you are changing. If the job is already obvious — a typo, a renamed variable, a single sentence — skip the plan and ask the agent to do it.

There is a second cost, and it is quieter. A written plan is less likely to skip something important. It is also less flexible when something unexpected turns up. That is why you review it before the work begins. If you approve every plan without reading it, you lose the only benefit planning gives you.

That also explains why a plan is different from a checklist you write for yourself. A checklist records what has worked before. A plan proposes what to do this time.

What to actually do

Ask for the plan before the work

On anything you haven’t done before, “Plan this first. Don’t change anything yet.” is the whole technique. It works in a plain chat window with no special mode or feature involved. You are asking the agent to propose a plan before it starts the work, and anyone can do that.

Look for one thing to question

Not as ceremony — plenty of plans are fine as they stand. The point is simply to check that you actually read it. If nothing in the plan is worth questioning, either it is a genuinely good plan or you skimmed it. It is worth knowing which one you just did.

Skip it when the job is small

Five steps for a renamed variable teach you nothing about whether planning helps, and they cost you time. Ask directly and get on with it.

Honesty corner
  • A plan isn’t a promise. Nothing about writing step four makes step four happen. The plan says what it means to do; only the run says what it did. Reading the first is not checking the second.
  • The first plan won’t survive contact. That isn’t a defect. It was written before the work began, when the agent knew the least about the job. The interesting question was never whether the first plan was perfect; it was whether it changed when it should have.
  • Reading the plan is the point. A longer plan is not automatically better, especially if nobody finishes reading it.

The next wall

Everything in this post depended on one thing.

There was only one plan.

The moment there are several agents, there is more than one plan.

That’s where multi-agent begins.

Sources & further reading
  • Choose a permission mode — Claude Code documentation. The load-bearing source for this page, under the heading “Analyze before you edit with plan mode”. The working details all live here rather than above, because they are the part most likely to be out of date by the time you read this: at the time of writing you enter plan mode with Shift+Tab, with /plan at the start of a message, or by launching with --permission-mode plan; the desktop app, the VS Code extension and claude.ai have a mode selector instead, so none of it needs a terminal; Ctrl+G opens the proposed plan in your text editor; and when a plan is ready you get four options, one of which is “No, keep planning”, which keeps you in plan mode and takes your correction. Two caveats from the same page worth knowing: version markers run all the way through it, and in a session you deliberately started with permission checks disabled, plan mode’s blocks are not enforced at all — the agent is still told to plan without editing, but nothing stops it.
  • Best practices for Claude Code — Anthropic. The section “Explore first, then plan, then code” is the short version of this whole article, written for people who already write code, and it is the source of the one-sentence test quoted above. Its opening line is the plainest reason planning exists: “Letting Claude jump straight to coding can produce code that solves the wrong problem.”
