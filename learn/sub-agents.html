A second conversation

30 July 2026

The email was fine.

That’s the part worth sitting with. You asked it to compare three website analytics tools, recommend one, and draft the email explaining why. It read pricing pages, feature lists, customer reviews, and documentation. It compared the trade-offs, ruled two of them out, and wrote an email that was good enough to send.

Then you ask for something completely different — how long to boil an egg.

All the work that went into choosing the analytics tool is still there. The pricing pages, the feature comparisons, the customer reviews, the twenty minutes of reading: all of it is still in the conversation, being read again on every turn while it tells you how long to boil an egg.

A conversation only adds.

There’s no putting anything down. The research isn’t being kept because anyone decided to keep it. It’s just how conversations work: once something goes in, it stays there.

So the question worth asking is whether that reading had to happen in your conversation at all.

So what is a second conversation?

It’s exactly what it sounds like. The agent opens another conversation alongside yours, and that one starts empty.

It gets one short instruction and nothing else. Not your history, not the files it already read, not the thing you mentioned twenty minutes ago. Anthropic’s documentation is blunt about it: each one “starts with a fresh, isolated context window”.

Then it does the work in its own conversation. It reads, searches, tries things, throws things away, and all of that stays where it is.

When it’s finished, one message comes back. Anthropic’s own worked example has the second conversation reading about 4,500 words of files. About 300 words came back.

The documentation calls this a sub-agent. That’s the term you’ll usually see. It’s a slightly grand name for a second conversation with one instruction going in and one answer coming out.

Why that helps

A job leaves behind far more than the answer — the pages it read, the searches it ran, the option it looked at for a minute and dropped.

It’s easier to see than to describe.

Bars are to scale. The reading dwarfs everything that crosses between the two, and it never leaves the panel it happened in.

Do the job in your conversation and the reading stays there. Do it in a second conversation, and it stays there instead.

The reading still happened. What changed is that you stopped paying for it again on every turn afterwards.

Same job, same reading, either way. The only thing that changes is which conversation is left holding it.

Do it here, and your conversation fills with everything it read, and stays that way. Send it away, and the reading piles up in the second conversation instead — which closes when the job is done, taking all of it. What crosses back is two thin bands: the instruction that went out, and the answer that came back.

Those two bands are the price. Next to a job this size they’re barely visible. Next to a one-line job they’d be most of the cost, which is why sending small work away tends to lose.

The work happens somewhere else. Only the answer comes back.

What to actually do

You don’t need a sub-agent to use the idea.

Open a fresh conversation. Do the messy work there — the reading, the comparing, the digging. Then bring one good answer back to the conversation you were already in.

That’s the whole trick.

Claude Code and Cowork — Anthropic’s two tools for working with an agent on your own files — automate this by opening and closing the second conversation for you. In ordinary chat, you can do exactly the same thing yourself.

The instruction is written for you

You ask your agent for something; your agent decides what the second conversation gets told. You don’t write that sentence and you don’t normally see it. Everything the second conversation knows comes from it, so the more you say in your ask, the more there is to pass on.

Sending small jobs away costs more than doing them

An instruction has to be written and read; an answer has to come back and be read again. On a job that would have taken a hundred words to do in place, that overhead is the whole cost and then some. The saving shows up when what gets left behind is much bigger than what comes back.

Three things are worth remembering.

  • Keep work you want to watch in your main conversation. If you’ll want to steer it as it goes, or to understand how it reached its answer, don’t send it somewhere else.
  • Ask for what you want back. If you want the shortlist, the numbers, or the reasoning, ask for them. Otherwise you’ll only get the final conclusion.
  • Go and look when it comes back wrong. The conversation that produced the answer hasn’t vanished. One you opened yourself is still sitting in your sidebar; one your agent opened can be picked up again where it stopped, if you ask for that. Reading what it actually did beats guessing at a better instruction.

The next wall

One sub-agent is easy to understand. The obvious next question is whether five are even better.

There’s real evidence for it. In June 2025 Anthropic published a system where several agents worked on one research question and beat a single agent by 90.2% on an internal test of research quality — a test they’ve never released, run on Claude Opus 4 and Claude Sonnet 4, both since superseded, burning around fifteen times the tokens of an ordinary chat.

Seven months later, the same company put out a much quieter page drawing a boundary around when that’s worth doing. Three situations, it says: when the reading piles up badly enough to hurt the work, when jobs can run at the same time, and when a narrower brief helps an agent pick the right tools and stay on task. “Outside these situations, the coordination costs typically exceed the benefits.”

The first of those three is this post. Sending the reading somewhere else is the case they kept.

One is broadly accepted. Five are still an active debate.

What makes a single second conversation work is that it can’t see anything else. Run five at once and that same blindness stops being useful.

They can’t see each other either. Two of them run the same search. The one gathering the material can’t ask the one writing the summary what it needs.

Sources & further reading
  • Sub-agents — Claude Code documentation: where the isolation described here comes from, plus the setup this post skips over. Written for people working in a terminal, and it carries version numbers — check the date before trusting any of it, including this post.
  • Context window — Claude Code documentation: the worked example behind the read-in and come-back figures. Counted there in tokens rather than words; this post converts.
  • How we built our multi-agent research system — Anthropic: the 90.2% and the fifteen-times figure, and a candid account of what went wrong on the way. The eval behind the headline number isn’t published.
  • When to use multi-agent systems (and when not to) — Anthropic: the three situations where the same company thinks several agents earn their keep, and the line it draws around them, January 2026. The single most useful page here if you’re deciding whether to bother.
  • Don’t Build Multi-Agents — Cognition: argued close to the opposite of the Anthropic post, at close to the same time. Its author, Walden Yan, narrowed the position in Multi-Agents: What’s Actually Working (April 2026): running several agents that all write at once still doesn’t work, he says, but other arrangements do. Worth reading alongside the original rather than instead of it.
  • Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail? — Cemri et al.: a Berkeley-led team read 150 recorded runs closely enough to sort the failures into fourteen kinds, then used that list to annotate more than 1,600 runs across seven frameworks. Useful mostly for how ordinary most of the failures turn out to be.
